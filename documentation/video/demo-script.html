0:00–2:40 Part I — introduction (the deck)

0:00–0:25 Slide 1 · Title + ethics

On screen

  • Slide 1; hold 2 s before speaking; let the ethics box be clearly readable

Voiceover

This is APEX — a behavioural driver-profiling system for Formula 1, built from public telemetry. On ethics: this project has no human participants — category A-zero — and uses only publicly available telemetry archives, in line with the University's ethical guidance.

0:25–0:50 Slide 2 · Motivation

On screen

  • Advance on "identical cars"

Voiceover

The motivation: two drivers in identical cars produce measurably different telemetry — throttle ramps, braking shape, cornering commitment. APEX turns those differences into a behavioural fingerprint, and asks the question the whole project set out to answer: does it encode who is driving, and does it generalise to circuits the system has never seen?

0:50–1:20 Slide 3 · Objectives → design

On screen

  • Slide 3; trace the funnel with the cursor

Voiceover

The original objectives dictated this shape directly. One funnel: telemetry is validated — every rejected lap coded, never silently dropped — features become the fingerprint, and everything downstream either validates it or applies it: gated identification, a style space, exact SHAP explanation, and two extended analyses. One design principle governed every layer from the specification onward: no component fails silently.

1:20–1:45 Slide 4 · The corpus

On screen

  • Slide 4; rest on the rose "2 held out" cell for the last line

Voiceover

Four seasons of real data — fifty-three thousand nine hundred clean lap vectors, thirteen drivers, twenty-five circuits. And the commitment that makes the results meaningful: Belgium and Italy were quarantined from the first line of code. Everything that follows is measured on circuits the models never saw.

1:45–2:15 Slide 5 · Conclusions — including the plan change

On screen

  • Slide 5; cursor across the strip, ending on the amber 0.78

Voiceover

The headline conclusions. On unseen circuits the fingerprint identifies the correct driver first sixty-three point six percent of the time — chance is under eight — and top-three eighty-four percent. And the project didn't entirely go to plan: the a-priori target of zero point seven eight was missed. The system reported that miss itself, and chasing it produced the project's best finding — a normalisation assumption that turned out to erase driver signal, whose correction is the model you're about to see serving.

2:15–2:40 Slides 6–9 · Confidence, structure, roadmap

On screen

  • Slide 6 (~8 s), slide 7 (~8 s), flash slide 8, land on slide 9

Voiceover

It only answers above a calibrated gate — right seventy-five percent of the time when it does. The fingerprint has statistically real structure — teammates cluster at p zero point zero two four — and it works as a live baseline, flagging deviation a median of nine laps before the pit stop. Now the software itself.

2:40–7:00 Part II — the software in use

2:40–3:50 Fingerprint → live identification

Screens: Driver Overview → LEC profile → Prediction (confident segment)

On screen

  • Cut to the Driver Overview; brief scroll; open LEC
  • Point at the radar, the signed SHAP weights, then 3,914 laps
  • Navigate to Prediction + Setup Tendencies; click the rehearsed confident segment; let the card fill; hover the confidence bar, then the truth line

Voiceover

This is the fingerprint in the product. Thirteen drivers, live from the real corpus — and here is Leclerc's: three thousand nine hundred laps in one signature, explained feature by feature with exact SHAP attributions. Committed throttle, aggressive late braking — that's what makes it his.

Now the experiment from the introduction, live. Each of these segments is a real lap from a held-out circuit — the classifier has never seen these tracks. Pick one… it names the driver, explains the decision, and states a calibrated confidence — calibrated meaning a zero point nine genuinely means ninety percent. And the truth line confirms it: correct.

3:50–4:30 The refusal, live

Screen: Prediction, gated segment (Belgium 2022 · Race · Lap 34)

On screen

  • Click the gated segment → the card turns epistemic purple
  • Trace the breakdown row: gate · distribution · model · overall
  • Point at "SHAP unavailable" and the empty setup panel; 2 s silence

Voiceover

And here's a different kind of interaction — one most classifiers can't have. This lap doesn't carry enough evidence, so APEX refuses: no driver named, a typed uncertainty state in its own colour, and the refusal decomposed — data quality, distribution familiarity, model confidence — so you can see which link was weak. Nothing downstream pretends otherwise: no fabricated explanation, no setup tendency. The whole interface obeys the gate.

4:30–5:15 Comparison — the geometry

Screen: Comparison, rehearsed teammate pair

On screen

  • Navigate to Comparison; pick the rehearsed teammates via chips
  • Sweep the shared-scale radars; the signed delta; hover one distinctive-feature bar for its plain-English meaning

Voiceover

The comparison view — a single analyst's tool, like every screen here, so there are no separate user roles to show. Two teammates side by side: same car, so what separates these fingerprints is mostly the drivers. Both radars share one scale per axis, and every distinctive feature is a signed SHAP difference the model computed — not a hand-picked statistic. This is the geometry behind that p-value, one pair at a time.

5:15–7:00 Replay — the fingerprint in time

Screen: Race Replay Monitor — single driver, then the 8-driver grid

On screen

  • Navigate to Replay; Bahrain 2023 · R, LEC, rehearsed speed, Play
  • Let the line wander under τ ~5 s in silence; the amber alert slides in at lap 15 — pause
  • Anatomy: confidence chip → laps-since-crossing → feature chips → historical pit window
  • Switch to ALL DRIVERS · 10× · Play; run to 26/26; hold the finished grid

Voiceover

The last interaction: the fingerprint as a baseline in time. This race is synthetic — a controlled drift scenario, so I know where the deviation is and you can watch the detector find it. Everything scoring it is the deployed model, replayed lap by lap, strictly causally — lap fifteen is scored knowing only laps one to fifteen, exactly what a live pit wall would have known.

Each lap the system measures how far the behaviour's shape has rotated from the driver's own baseline; the dashed line is his threshold, calibrated per driver, per circuit type. Two consecutive crossings — and there it is. High confidence, the exact features driving it — a decomposition that sums to the score, not an estimate — and a pit window stated as what it is: a historical median, not a recommendation.

Evaluated over two thousand one hundred real stints it leads the stop by a median of nine laps — and, reported honestly, ninety-one percent of alerts self-resolve: this detects behavioural deviation, transients included, with degradation as one driver of it.

And it scales — eight drivers, one race, every monitor in lock-step.

7:00–8:50 Part III — the development environment and the code

7:00–7:30 The environment

Screen: VS Code — repo tree · terminal with the green suite · config.yaml

On screen

  • Cut to VS Code; sidebar shows the repo (app/ mirrored by tests/)
  • Show the terminal: the tail of the full pytest -q run, green
  • Open the pinned config.yaml at the thresholds: block

Voiceover

Under the hood. The repository mirrors the pipeline — and every source package is shadowed by a test package: one thousand two hundred and fifty tests, and the rule that every error code has a test that triggers it. One more convention worth showing: there are no magic numbers anywhere in the code. Every threshold you saw — the confidence gate, the lap minimums — lives here, in one typed configuration file.

7:30–8:00 Code I · the confidence gate

Screen: app/ml/inference.py, the gate (~line 457)

On screen

  • Switch to the pinned inference.py tab; the entropy → confidence → accepted cascade fills the screen; cursor on the two state = lines

Voiceover

Three functions I'd call the heart of the system. First, the gate you watched refuse. High entropy — ambiguous; below the calibrated threshold — low confidence. In both branches the prediction stays empty, the state is a served value rather than an exception, and the exact margin is logged. That purple card earlier is these lines reaching the screen.

8:00–8:25 Code II · the leakage tripwire

Screen: app/pipeline/normalise.py, _assert_no_leakage (~line 206)

On screen

  • Switch to normalise.py; the tripwire on screen; cursor on the raise

Voiceover

Second — the line every held-out number depends on. Before any statistic is fitted, this tripwire checks whether a quarantined circuit survived the exclusion filter; if one did, it raises, and no artefact is written. Every fit site re-asserts this boundary independently. The results are only credible because this line can fire — and it's tested to.

8:25–8:50 Code III · the deviation score

Screen: app/monitoring/detector.py, _cosine_distance (~line 536)

On screen

  • Switch to detector.py; cursor on the terms = 0.5 * (…)**2 line

Voiceover

And the piece I'm proudest of: the monitor's score. A cosine distance — the shape of the behaviour, not its magnitude, so fuel burn doesn't read as degradation — and this one line is a unit-vector identity that makes the per-feature attribution exact. The feature chips on the alert banner sum to the score, to floating-point tolerance.

8:50–9:20 Close — Slide 10

On screen

  • Cut to the deck, slide 10; hold the final frame ~3 s after the last word

Voiceover

A behavioural fingerprint from public data. Identification that generalises — with its target miss reported and turned into the finding that improved the served model. A style space with real structure; a monitor that watches it live. And one principle from the first telemetry sample to the last pixel: the system would rather tell you it doesn't know than pretend that it does. This is APEX.
